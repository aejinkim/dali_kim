# Cross: Blockchain Remittance UX for Migrant Workers in Korea

As **Product Designer** at Coinone Transfer (Jan 2018 – Jan 2019), I owned the mobile/web product for **Cross**, a government-certified cross-border remittance service, solo, end-to-end, from user research and wireframing through frontend implementation handoff.

---

## 🎯 Context

Korea has a large and growing population of foreign residents. Migrant workers from Vietnam, the Philippines, Thailand, Indonesia, and more, alongside international students and long-term expats. For this population, sending money home is not a convenience feature. It is a monthly obligation tied to family survival.

The existing options were painful:
- **Banks.** High fees, long wait times, complex Korean-language paperwork, and rejection risk for users without full Korean banking credentials.

Coinone Transfer saw an opportunity to use Ripple's blockchain settlement to bypass the correspondent banking layer, dramatically cutting cost and time. But the technology was the easy part. **The hard part was designing a mobile product a Vietnamese factory worker could trust with their family's rent money**, in a language and interface they could navigate confidently.

---

## 🧭 Challenge

The Constraints:
1. Users were sending money that mattered. Any confusion (an unclear fee, an ambiguous status, a hidden step) would break trust permanently.
2. KYC required official identity verification, but many users had expired passports, no Korean bank account, or limited Korean address history.
3. **Language and literacy diversity.** Users spoke multiple languages, had wide-ranging digital literacy, and often used shared or older Android devices.

---

## 🛠️ Design Direction

Rather than optimizing for the "average user," I designed for the **most constrained user first**: a first-time sender, on an older Android phone, with limited Korean, sending money on a payday deadline. Everything cascaded from there:

- **Radical simplicity in the transfer flow.** Recipient → amount → confirm. Everything else was hidden until needed.
- **Fixed, transparent fee** (5,000 KRW to any country). No exchange rate anxiety, no hidden math. Trust through predictability.
- **Status as story, not code.** Transaction states were designed as human-language milestones ("Sent to bank", "Arriving in Vietnam"), not technical statuses.
- **KYC as a conversation, not a form.** Each verification step explained why it existed, with fallback paths for expired documents.
- **Multi-language from day one.** Designed with variable text lengths and cultural conventions in mind, not translated afterward.
- **Referral loop tuned to community trust.** Foreign worker communities in Korea rely heavily on word-of-mouth, so referrals were designed to feel like recommending a friend, not earning points.

---

## 📈 Impact

- Cross has continued operating for **7+ years** since launch (2018 → present), a rare feat of longevity in Korean fintech.
- The company evolved into an independent entity, **CROSS ENF Inc.**, expanding scope into Cross Shop, a K-goods commerce experience for foreigners in Korea.
- Product remains active on both **Google Play** and the **App Store**.
- Retains its **government registration** (Small Amount Overseas Remittance Business No. 2018-11) as one of the earlier certified operators in Korea.
- Design foundations laid in 2018 remain visible in the current product.

---

## 💡 What I Learned

- **Designing for high-stakes financial trust is different from designing for engagement.** The goal isn't to maximize interaction. It's to make each interaction feel safe enough to repeat next month.
- **Regulation is a design constraint, not a design ceiling.** The most restrictive requirements often produced the clearest UX, as forcing KYC transparency became a trust asset.
- **Underserved user segments teach you the most.** Designing for foreign workers in Korea forced me to unlearn assumptions about digital literacy, language, and device context.
- **Blockchain works best when it's invisible.** Users didn't need to know Ripple existed. They needed to know the money arrived. The design's job was to make the infrastructure disappear.
